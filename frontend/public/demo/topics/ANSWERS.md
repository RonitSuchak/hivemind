## English: apostrophes for possession (`english-apostrophes`)

| Question | Correct option | Why |
|---|---|---|
| q1 | friend | The spelling is friend (i before e here). |
| q2 | there | There means in that place. Their means belonging to them. They're means they are. |
| q3 | The dog barked at the postman. | It starts with a capital letter and ends with a full stop. Postman is not a name so it needs no capital. |
| q4 | We bought apples, pears and grapes. | Commas separate the items in a list. There is no comma straight after bought and no comma around and. |
| q5 | quickly | Quickly tells us how the cat climbed, so it is an adverb. Climbed is a verb, cat and tree are nouns. |
| q6 | Only Sam passed the test. | Nobody except Sam means Sam was the only person who passed. |
| q7 | the teacher's coat | One owner, so add 's after teacher. |
| q8 | the sisters' room | Many owners ending in s, so the apostrophe goes after the s. |
| q9 | its | Its shows ownership and takes no apostrophe. It's would mean it is, and "the bird built it is nest" makes no sense. |
| q10 | It's | It's means it is, and "It is time to go home" makes sense. |
| q11 | The tree lost its leaves, and it's cold now. | Its (ownership) has no apostrophe. It's (it is) has one. The first its is ownership, the second is it is. |

## Economics: price elasticity of demand (`economics-elasticity`)

| Question | Correct option | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ec-a1 | It falls | A higher price means buyers choose to buy less, so there is a movement up the curve. |
| ec-a2 | A rise in consumers' incomes | Own-price changes cause movements along the curve. A change in income shifts the curve, and a rise in income shifts a normal good's demand right. |
| ec-a3 | The demand curve for coffee shifts to the left | Cheaper tea pulls buyers away from coffee at every price, so coffee demand shifts left. |
| ec-a4 | There is a shortage of 30 units, so the price is likely to rise | Demand exceeds supply: 120 − 90 = 30 units short, so sellers can raise the price. |
| ec-a5 | −20% | Change ÷ original = (4 − 5) ÷ 5 = −0.2, which is −20%. (−25% divides by the new price.) |
| ec-b1 | 2.5 | 25 ÷ 10 = 2.5. Check: 10 × 2.5 = 25. (0.4 inverts the ratio.) |
| ec-b2 | A fall of 4% | % change in quantity = PED size × % change in price = 0.4 × 10 = 4%, and price up means quantity down. (25% inverts the ratio.) |
| ec-b3 | It rises, because quantity falls by a smaller percentage than price rises | Size 0.6 is below 1, so demand is inelastic and a price rise raises revenue. |
| ec-b4 | PED is −0.5, so demand is inelastic, and revenue rises | Price: +2 ÷ 20 = +10%. Quantity: −20 ÷ 400 = −5%. PED = −5 ÷ 10 = −0.5. Revenue: £20 × 400 = £8,000 rises to £22 × 380 = £8,360. |
| ec-r1 | The pool is unsafe and might close within a year | Stated in the passage: others pointed out that the pool is unsafe and might have to close within a year. |
| ec-r2 | It was put off until next year | Stated: the plan was not cancelled and was put off until next year. |
| ec-r3 | The library will not be built this year | The council cannot afford both, it chose the pool, and the library was put off until next year. |
| ec-l1 | Weigh up the effects, with points for and against, and reach a judgement | Evaluate means to judge, which needs both sides and a conclusion. |
| ec-l2 | State | State means give the answer briefly. The others ask for reasons or several points. |
| ec-l3 | At a price of £5, demand for concert tickets is 300 a week. | Only the first option links a quantity bought to a price, which is the economics meaning. |
| ec-c1 | Falls by £992 | Quantity falls 8 × 1.5 = 12%, so 800 becomes 704. Price becomes £27. New revenue = 27 × 704 = £19,008. Old revenue = £20,000, a fall of £992. Check: 1.08 × 0.88 = 0.9504, and 0.9504 × 20,000 = 19,008. |

## Further Maths: the complex conjugate and dividing (`further-maths-complex`)

| Question | Correct option | Why |
|---|---|---|
| fm-a1 | −9 | (3i)² = 9 × i² = 9 × (−1) = −9. |
| fm-a2 | −2 | The imaginary part is the number multiplying i, including its sign, so −2. |
| fm-a3 | 6 − 2i | Add real parts: 4 + 2 = 6. Add imaginary parts: 3 − 5 = −2. So 6 − 2i. |
| fm-a4 | −10 + 11i | 2 + 8i + 3i + 12i² = 2 + 11i − 12 = −10 + 11i. (14 + 11i forgets that i² = −1.) |
| fm-a5 | x = −2 ± 3i | Discriminant 16 − 52 = −36, so x = (−4 ± 6i) ÷ 2 = −2 ± 3i. Check: (−2 + 3i)² + 4(−2 + 3i) + 13 = (−5 − 12i) + (−8 + 12i) + 13 = 0. |
| fm-b1 | 6 + 5i | Keep the real part and flip the sign of the imaginary part. |
| fm-b2 | 25 | A number times its conjugate is a² + b² = 16 + 9 = 25. Check: 16 − 12i + 12i − 9i² = 16 + 9 = 25. |
| fm-b3 | −1 + 2i | Multiply by (1 + i) over (1 + i): top = 1 + i + 3i + 3i² = −2 + 4i, bottom = 1 + 1 = 2, so −1 + 2i. Check: (−1 + 2i)(1 − i) = −1 + i + 2i − 2i² = 1 + 3i. |
| fm-b4 | z = 3 − i | z = (5 + 5i) ÷ (1 + 2i). Multiply by (1 − 2i): top = 5 − 10i + 5i − 10i² = 15 − 5i, bottom = 5, so 3 − i. Check: (1 + 2i)(3 − i) = 3 − i + 6i − 2i² = 5 + 5i. |
| fm-r1 | Squaring an ordinary number never gives a negative answer | Stated in the first two sentences. |
| fm-r2 | The roots cancelled before the end | Stated: they watched the roots cancel before the end. |
| fm-r3 | Their real solutions could be reached by working through square roots of negative numbers | The passage says the equations had real solutions and that the roots were needed in the working but cancelled out. |
| fm-l1 | Use your answer to the first part to help with the second | Hence means use the previous result. |
| fm-l2 | Working that leads step by step to 5 | The result is given, so marks are for the working that reaches it. |
| fm-l3 | The complex number 2 + 5i has a real part of 2. | Only the first option is about a number with real and imaginary parts. |
| fm-c1 | k = 6 | Top: (3 + ki)(1 − 2i) = 3 − 6i + ki − 2ki² = (3 + 2k) + (k − 6)i. Imaginary part zero gives k = 6. Check: (3 + 6i) ÷ (1 + 2i) = 3, which is real. |

## Physics: projectile motion (`physics-projectiles`)

| Question | Correct option | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ph-a1 | Velocity | Velocity has a direction as well as a size. The others are scalars. |
| ph-a2 | 19.6 m | s = ut + ½at² = 0 + ½ × 9.81 × 2.0² = 19.6 m. Check: v = 19.6 m s⁻¹ at 2.0 s, average 9.81 m s⁻¹, × 2.0 s = 19.6 m. |
| ph-a3 | 2.0 s | v = u + at gives 0 = 19.6 − 9.81t, so t = 2.0 s. (4.0 s is the time for the whole up and down trip.) |
| ph-a4 | 3.0 m s⁻² | a = change in velocity ÷ time = (25 − 10) ÷ 5.0 = 3.0 m s⁻². |
| ph-a5 | 17.3 m s⁻¹ | 20 cos 30° = 17.3 m s⁻¹. Check: √(20² − 10²) = √300 = 17.3. (10 uses sin instead of cos.) |
| ph-b1 | It stays at 2.0 m s⁻¹ | No horizontal force acts, so the horizontal velocity is constant. |
| ph-b2 | They hit the ground at the same time | Both start with zero vertical velocity and fall the same height with the same acceleration g, so the fall time is the same. |
| ph-b3 | 10 m | 19.6 = ½ × 9.81 × t² gives t = 2.0 s. Distance = 5.0 × 2.0 = 10 m. Check: ½ × 9.81 × 2.0² = 19.6 m. |
| ph-b4 | 20.5 m s⁻¹ | Vertical velocity = 9.81 × 2.0 = 19.6 m s⁻¹. Speed = √(6.0² + 19.6²) = √(36 + 384.9) = 20.5 m s⁻¹. (25.6 adds the components instead of using Pythagoras.) |
| ph-r1 | There is almost no air to push back against the feather | Stated: the Moon has almost no air, so nothing pushed back against the feather. |
| ph-r2 | The push of air against something moving through it | Stated: air pushes up against anything that falls through it, and this push is called air resistance. |

| ph-l1 | A rough graph with labelled axes and the correct shape | Sketch means a freehand graph showing the key features, not exact plotting. |
| ph-l2 | Show step by step how the expression follows from known equations | Derive means show the steps that lead to a result. |
